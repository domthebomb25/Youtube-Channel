01

Title Patterns

27 of 28 titles are questions ending in “?” — even ones that are grammatically statements (“Why Trench Foot Was More Dangerous Than Bullets?”). Openers: Why ×16, How ×7, What ×3, Who ×1, Do ×1. Average length: 8.4 words.

  • The paradox: the title states something that sounds backwards — more clothes in heat, cold water making you hotter, spicy food keeping you cool. Both breakouts and the #3 video (11K) use this.
  • Extreme number: 45°C, 50°C, Minus 40 Degree — puts a vivid stake in the title.
  • A group of people as the subject: Desert People, Ancient Egyptians, Mongolian Nomads, Romans — not “humans” in general.
  • “Without modern X”: Without Air Conditioning / Before Air Conditioning / In a Tent / With No Houses.
  • “Yours” comparison: “Why Your House Is Hotter Than a Roman Villa?” pulls the viewer into the question.
  • “Actually”: signals a myth about to be busted (“Actually Sleep”, “Actually Keeps You Cool”).

02

Thumbnail Analysis

One locked recipe across every video:

  • Style: hand-drawn cartoon with thick black ink outlines and soft painted color (“Inkly”). No real faces, no photos.
  • Character: a recurring stick-figure mascot — round white head, huge googly eyes, simple line body. Ancient people are the same character in costume (turban, pharaoh headdress, laurel wreath).
  • Text: top edge, 2–3 words, ALL CAPS, bright yellow with a heavy black outline, always ending in “?” — a provocative fragment, never the title (“MORE CLOTHES?!”, “50°C NIGHTS?”, “HOT TEA WINS?”, “COOL SECRET?”).
  • Emotion: extreme — the “modern” character is melting, sweating, tongue out, eyes bulging; the “ancient” one is smug, relaxed, sipping tea.
  • Composition: a suffering-vs-smug face-off (often a split with a jagged lightning crack for “MODERN vs. ANCIENT?”), or a single sufferer with one giant prop.
  • Props and palette: red thermometer, angry sun, sweat drops, heat squiggles, camels. Saturated orange/red for heat, cool blues for the “clever” side and for cold topics.
03

Hook Structures

Three opening moves, each built from short, punchy fragments:

1 · Second-person immersion → “this was normal”

“Picture yourself in ancient Egypt at noon in summer. The sun is directly overhead. Over a hundred degrees… No air conditioning. No ice water. No escape. This wasn't a rare brutal day. This was just summer.”— How Did Ancient Egyptians Survive Egypt's Brutal Heat? (opening narration, from description)

2 · Correct the premise, then sharpen the question

“You've eaten a predator. Tuna is a predator. Salmon hunts. So the question isn't why we can't eat predators — we can. The real question is why land predators almost never end up on a plate.”— Why Don't Humans Eat Predators? (3.9× median)

3 · Name the paradox in the first chapter

First chapters are literally titled “The Counterintuitive Logic of Desert Attire” (2.2M), “The cold water paradox” (11K), “The challenge of desert heat” (797K). The hook restates the backwards-sounding claim, adds a sensory picture of the heat, then promises the reason.

Breakout hook, second by second (2.2M video): 0:00 “Here's something that makes absolute no sense the first time you hear it.” → 0:04–0:14 sensory stakes (120°F, “the sun is a physical weight”) → 0:15 the backwards fact (“They do the opposite. They cover up.”) → 0:26 named peoples (Bedouin, Tuareg) for credibility → 0:37 “And your instinct screams that this is insane.” → 0:51 the question restated → 1:02 the promise (“a beautiful lesson in how heat actually works”) → 1:19 “Here's why.” The paradox is stated three times in the first minute.

04

Script Format

  1. Cold open (0:00–~1:15): paradox + sensory heat picture + “the answer isn't what you think”.
  2. Set up the enemy (~1:15–3:20): name the physical forces — e.g. “Identifying the Two Primary Heat Enemies” (direct sun vs. hot air).
  3. Mechanism blocks (3–5 chapters): one technique per chapter, each explained with simple physics — evaporation, thermal mass, airflow, the “bellows effect”.
  4. Twist chapter near the end: a second paradox inside the first — “The Mystery of Wearing Black in the Sun”, “Why cold can make you hotter”.
  5. Close: a legacy/lesson for the modern viewer — “A legacy of ingenuity”, “Lessons for the modern world”.
  • Chapters: 5–9 per video, with descriptive titles, every 1–2.5 min.
  • Credibility layer: every description ends with an academic SOURCES block (journal papers, university press books), each with a one-line note on what it supports.
  • Voice: calm, confident, slightly wry narrator; short declarative sentences; repetition for rhythm (“Not one trick. An entire way of life.”). No on-screen host.
  • Calls to action: no CTA appears in the descriptions or chapter titles; the channel leans on topic pull, not asks.
05

Pacing & Story Arc

Length: settled on 10–11 minutes (the 2.2M hit is 10:12); the 20-minute Egypt format hit once but was dropped. Upload cadence: about one video every 1–2 days since mid-September.

Arc: myth → stakes → mechanism → mechanism → twist → payoff for you. Each chapter answers one sub-question and opens the next (“but that only works when there's wind…”), so the viewer always has an open loop.

Measured on the 2.2M video: ~2,070 words in 10:11 — a brisk ~190–200 wpm (from the scene-analysis narration, so treat as approximate). Sentences average ~15 words; “you/your” appears 75 times — the viewer is constantly placed inside the scene. Visuals change every 2–4 seconds: each narration beat gets 2–4 quick cartoon panels (diagrams with arrows, side-by-side comparisons, crossed-out “wrong” answers, ✓ vs ✗). A recurring narrator mascot — the stick figure standing in a green field — cuts in roughly every 20–40 seconds to react (confused, pointing up, excited) and reset attention. Signature transitions: “But here's the problem…”, “Now watch what the robe does.”, “And the design gets even cleverer…”, “Now this brings us to one of the most famous and puzzling details of all…”. Ending: a full recap (“So let's put the whole picture together…”), a moral (“human instinct isn't always right”), then a “next time you see…” callback that restates the title's answer — wear more, not less.

06

What Makes It Work

The replicable mechanism: an “obviously wrong” survival fact about a vivid extreme, answered with simple physics, sold by a smug-vs-suffering cartoon.

  • The curiosity gap is in the title itself. “More clothes in heat?” sounds wrong, so you have to click to resolve it.
  • Heat is universal and seasonal. Every viewer has been too hot; both hits went out in August/September.
  • The thumbnail tells the whole story in one second: modern-you suffering, the ancient person smug. The viewer wants to be the smug one.
  • Evergreen and searchable. “Why do desert people wear more clothes” is a question people actually type.
  • A cheap, consistent visual system (one mascot, one font treatment, flat cartoon art) makes daily uploads possible.
